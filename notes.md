# Real Estate Professional Status (REPS) and Related Tax Savings

## Executive Summary
Real Estate Professional Status (REPS) under IRC §469(c)(7) lets a qualifying taxpayer treat rental real estate losses as non-passive, so they can offset wages and business income. The taxpayer must pass two tests: more than half of personal services in all trades or businesses must be in real property trades or businesses where they materially participate, and they must log more than 750 hours in those activities. Without REPS, rental losses are generally passive, with a narrow $25,000 active-participation allowance that phases out at higher income. A related path is the short-term rental exception (average stay of 7 days or less), which can avoid the rental-activity classification without REPS. Depreciation, including cost segregation and 100% bonus depreciation, often amplifies the losses. Hour documentation is the main audit risk, and the case law is strict. This is general information, not tax advice.

## 1. The REPS Test (IRC §469(c)(7))

The test is in §469(c)(7)(B). A taxpayer qualifies for a year if both conditions are met[^1]:
- **More-than-half test:** more than half of personal services in all trades or businesses are in real property trades or businesses (RPTBs) where the taxpayer materially participates[^1].
- **750-hour test:** more than 750 hours of services are performed in RPTBs where the taxpayer materially participates[^1].

On a joint return, one spouse must separately meet both tests[^1].

Treas. Reg. §1.469-9 adds detail[^2]:
- RPTBs include development, construction, acquisition, conversion, rental, operation, management, leasing, and brokerage[^2].
- Material participation in each RPTB is required for those hours to count[^2].
- W-2 employee hours generally do not count unless the taxpayer is a 5% owner of the employer[^2].

**Grouping election (§1.469-9(g))**[^2][^3]:
- By default, each rental interest is a separate activity for material participation.
- A qualifying taxpayer may elect to treat all rental real estate interests as one activity. This pools hours across properties.
- A grouping election involving limited partnership interests triggers stricter tests under §1.469-9(f).

## 2. Why REPS Saves Tax

**Passive vs. non-passive**
- Rental activities are per se passive under §469. Passive losses can only offset passive income[^4].
- REPS removes rentals from the automatic passive category. Material participation is then tested activity by activity (or under a grouping election). Qualifying losses become non-passive and can offset wages and business income[^5].

**$25,000 special allowance (§469(i))**
- Taxpayers who actively participate (at least 10% ownership and bona fide management decisions) may deduct up to $25,000 of rental losses against non-passive income[^5].
- The allowance is reduced by 50% of MAGI over $100,000 and is eliminated at $150,000[^5].
- Unlike REPS, the allowance is capped and phases out. REPS has no dollar cap or income phase-out, but requires the hour tests[^5].

**Short-term rental exception (Treas. Reg. §1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A))**
- If the average customer stay is 7 days or less, the activity is not a "rental activity"[^6].
- It becomes an ordinary trade or business. Losses are non-passive if the taxpayer materially participates under the seven §1.469-5T tests (for example, 500+ hours, or substantially all of the participation)[^6][^7].
- This route does not require the REPS 750-hour or more-than-half tests. It is an independent carve-out, so REPS is not needed[^6].

## 3. Depreciation Amplifies the Loss

- **Residential rental property:** 27.5-year straight-line recovery (80%+ of gross rent from dwelling units). **Nonresidential:** 39-year straight-line. Both use the mid-month convention[^8].
- **Cost segregation:** reclassifies building components into 5, 7, and 15-year property. Those amounts become eligible for bonus depreciation. The IRS publishes a cost segregation audit guide, Publication 5653[^9].
- **Bonus depreciation (2026):** Pub. 946 says the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21) reinstated 100% bonus depreciation for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025. The 2026 figure is inferred from this, since no explicit "2026: 100%" IRS statement was located[^10].
- **Qualified property** is tangible MACRS property with a recovery period of 20 years or less[^10].

Combined effect: a cost segregation study plus 100% bonus depreciation can generate large first-year losses on a property. REPS is what lets those losses offset wages or business income.

## 4. Audit Risk and Case Law

**Documentation standard**
- The hour tests are fact-intensive. Logs should be contemporaneous, showing date, hours, property, and task[^11].
- Practitioner guidance warns against round or fixed hour entries, on-call time, and logs reconstructed after an audit notice[^11].
- These are secondary, non-IRS sources[^11].

**Key cases (holdings from secondary summaries; opinions not fetched)**
- **Moss v. Commissioner, 135 T.C. 365 (2010):** the court reportedly held that on-call availability is not "services performed." Only actual services count[^12].
- **Lee v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2006-193:** the court reportedly found the claimed hours unreliable and held that material participation was not proved[^13].
- **Escalante v. Commissioner, T.C. Summary Op. 2015-47:** the taxpayer was a full-time teacher. The court reportedly held the hours were insufficient. Summary opinions are non-precedential[^14].

**Common audit red flags** (practitioner commentary, not an IRS checklist)[^15]:
- Logs created after an audit notice
- Repeated fixed-hour entries
- Counting on-call time
- A full-time W-2 job making the more-than-half test implausible
- No corroborating records such as calendars, emails, or invoices

## 5. Practical Checklist
1. Confirm more-than-half and 750-hour tests with contemporaneous logs.
2. Decide whether to make the §1.469-9(g) grouping election. Consider the limited-partnership caveat.
3. Consider the short-term rental path if average stays are 7 days or less. It may avoid REPS entirely.
4. Model cost segregation and bonus depreciation against projected income.
5. Keep corroborating records for every claimed hour.
6. Consult a CPA or tax attorney before relying on these positions.
